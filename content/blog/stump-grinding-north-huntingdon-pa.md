We hear it pretty often after a tree removal: "Just leave the stump, we'll deal with it later." Sometimes that means the homeowner is planning to rent a grinder and handle it themselves. Sometimes it just means they don't want to spend the extra money that day. Either way, the stump stays.

We get it. Stump grinding feels like an optional add-on after you've already written a check for tree removal. The tree is gone, the immediate problem is solved, and the stump isn't hurting anything.

Except it usually is. Here's what we've actually seen happen when stumps are left in Western PA yards.

![Old stump left in a residential yard with new growth sprouting around the base](https://picsum.photos/id/75/800/500)

## The Stump Won't Just Rot Away in a Few Years

The most common misconception is that a stump will decay and disappear on its own within a season or two. That's not how it works. Hardwood stumps — oak, maple, black locust, which make up most of what's in yards around Westmoreland County — take anywhere from **10 to 20 years** to fully decompose at ground level. Softwoods are faster, but you're still looking at 5 to 10 years.

During that entire time, the stump is sitting in your yard rotting at whatever pace the weather, soil moisture, and wood-decaying fungi dictate. It doesn't shrink noticeably for the first several years. It just sits there, getting uglier and more hollow every spring.

## Certain Species Will Grow Right Back

This catches people off guard. If the tree was a black locust, a sumac, a silver maple, or an aspen, leaving the stump means you're not done dealing with the tree. These species resprout aggressively from the root system and stump after being cut down. You'll have a ring of new shoots growing up around the stump within weeks of removal.

Black locust is especially persistent. We've seen customers mow those shoots down repeatedly for two and three years before giving up and calling us. By then, some of the sprouts had gotten several feet tall and were starting to look like actual trees again.

Stump grinding removes enough of the stump and root flare that regrowth is usually stopped or at least manageable. Leaving it in place and hoping the species won't resprout is a coin flip you'll often lose.

## Carpenter Ants and Termites Move In Fast

Decaying wood is exactly what carpenter ants are looking for. They don't eat wood the way termites do — they excavate it to build galleries and nesting chambers. A soft, partially rotted stump in your yard is an ideal spot, and once a colony establishes, they don't stay contained to the stump.

In Western PA, carpenter ants are active from late spring through early fall and they forage up to 100 yards from the nest. We've talked to homeowners who traced a carpenter ant problem in their garage or deck framing back to a stump sitting 30 feet away. Termites are less common in Westmoreland County than further south, but they're not unheard of, and a rotting stump close to your home's foundation is the last thing you want to leave in place.

## Fungal Disease Can Spread to Your Other Trees

Wood-decaying fungi — armillaria (honey fungus) in particular — colonize dead stumps and can spread through the soil along root systems to neighboring live trees. This is more of a concern in landscapes where multiple trees share a root zone, like a row of oaks or a mixed planting along a property line.

Armillaria is common in Pennsylvania. It's identifiable by clusters of honey-colored mushrooms appearing at the base of the stump in fall, and by the white mycelial mats under the bark. Once it's in your soil, it doesn't go away — but removing the stump and as much of the root flare as possible eliminates the primary food source and slows the spread significantly.

## It Becomes a Mowing Problem

Anyone who's mowed around a stump for a season or two knows how much time it wastes. You're trimming around it by hand because the mower can't get close, or you're bouncing over surface roots that radiate out from the base. The grass directly around the stump tends to go patchy because the decaying root system is pulling nutrients as it breaks down.

Low stumps — ones that were cut close to the ground — are actually more dangerous than tall ones for mowing. Hit one of those with a riding mower at the right angle and you can throw debris or damage the deck.

## What Stump Grinding Actually Involves

Stump grinding uses a rotating cutting wheel with carbide teeth to chew through the stump and the root flare below grade. We typically grind 6 to 12 inches below the surface, which is deep enough for grass or plantings to establish over the area.

The machine leaves behind a pile of wood chips and sawdust — the grindings. These can be:

- **Left in place and used as mulch** in nearby beds (they'll break down over a season or two)
- **Raked back into the hole** and topped with topsoil for replanting lawn
- **Hauled away** if you'd rather start clean

One thing worth knowing: the grindings are high in carbon and will tie up nitrogen as they decompose. If you're filling the hole and replanting grass over it, mix in some topsoil rather than just leaving straight wood chips at root depth. The grass will establish much better.

## When Can You Plant After Stump Grinding?

You can plant grass seed directly over a ground stump area once the hole is filled with a topsoil/grindings mix. Give it a few weeks for the soil to settle before seeding, and expect to top-dress and reseed again the following spring as the remaining grindings beneath the surface continue to break down and settle.

For shrubs or ornamental plantings, same principle — fill the void, let it settle, plant. Avoid putting a new tree directly in the same spot for at least a year. The remaining root decay and any fungal activity in the old root zone can affect a new tree planted too soon in the same location.
