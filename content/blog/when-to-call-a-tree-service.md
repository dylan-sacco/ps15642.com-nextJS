Trees are one of the most valuable features of any Westmoreland County property. A mature shade tree can add thousands of dollars to your home's value, lower your cooling costs, and provide decades of beauty. But a diseased, dead, or structurally compromised tree is a liability — one that can damage your home, injure someone, or fall on a neighbor's property.

The challenge is knowing when a tree needs professional attention and when it can wait. Most homeowners don't notice warning signs until it's too late, and by then, the repair bill is far higher than the cost of proactive service would have been.

Here are seven warning signs that it's time to call a professional tree service — and why you shouldn't wait.

![Large mature tree with potential hazard limbs overhanging a residential home](https://picsum.photos/id/57/800/500)

## 1. Dead or Hanging Branches

Dead branches — called "widow makers" by arborists — are the most immediate hazard in any tree. A dead branch can fall at any time, with no warning and no wind required. The wood becomes brittle, loses its connection to the tree's vascular system, and eventually snaps.

**Signs a branch is dead:**
- No leaves during growing season while surrounding branches have full foliage
- Brittle, dry bark that falls off easily
- The branch feels light and hollow compared to healthy wood

Any large dead branch over a structure, vehicle, or area where people walk should be removed immediately.

## 2. A Leaning Tree That Wasn't Leaning Before

Some trees grow with a natural lean — that's normal and generally stable. What's dangerous is a tree that has *recently* started to lean, especially after a storm or heavy rain. This indicates:

- Root failure or root rot on the uphill side
- Soil heaving or erosion undermining the root plate
- Wind damage that has compromised the tree's anchor

If you notice a tree that has shifted position, especially if you see exposed roots or cracked soil at the base, call a tree service for an assessment right away. This is a potential emergency.

![A tree leaning dangerously toward a house after storm damage](https://picsum.photos/id/360/800/500)

## 3. Cracks or Splits in the Trunk

Deep, vertical cracks in a tree trunk — called seams — indicate structural weakness that could cause the tree to split apart under stress. This is particularly common in trees with multiple trunks (co-dominant stems) where the union at the base can become weak over time.

Look for:
- Cracks that penetrate deep into the wood (not just surface bark damage)
- Included bark — where bark is trapped between two stems, creating a structurally weak union
- Discoloration or seeping around a crack, indicating decay inside

A certified arborist can evaluate whether cabling, bracing, or removal is the right solution.

## 4. Hollow Trunk or Mushrooms at the Base

Fungi growing at the base of a tree — particularly shelf fungi or mushrooms — are a sign of internal decay. Wood-decaying fungi consume the structural wood inside the trunk, hollowing it from the inside out while the outside may still look relatively healthy.

A hollow trunk doesn't always mean immediate removal, but it requires professional evaluation. Arborists use tools like resistographs (drilling instruments) to measure how much sound wood remains and calculate whether the tree is still structurally viable.

## 5. Epicormic Shoots (Stress Sprouts)

Epicormic shoots — the clusters of small, weak shoots that sprout from the lower trunk or major branches — are a tree's stress response. They indicate the tree is under significant duress and trying to generate new photosynthetic capacity to survive.

Common causes include:
- Severe root damage (from construction, soil compaction, or grade changes)
- Disease or pest infestation
- Extreme drought or flooding

These shoots are a cry for help. Have a professional assess the underlying cause before the tree declines further.

## 6. Storm Damage

After any significant storm, walk your property and inspect your trees. Storm damage that requires immediate attention includes:

- **Hanging or partially attached branches** — these can fall suddenly even days after a storm
- **Split trunks** — where the storm has begun separating the tree into pieces
- **Uprooted root balls** — even a partially uprooted tree is unstable
- **Branches on power lines** — never attempt to remove these yourself; call your utility company

Even trees that look mostly intact after a storm may have hidden internal cracks or compromised root systems. A professional inspection after major storms is a smart investment.

![Storm damage to a large tree in a suburban backyard](https://picsum.photos/id/177/800/500)

## 7. The Tree Is Too Close to Your Home or Power Lines

Sometimes the concern isn't the health of the tree — it's the location. Trees that have grown too close to your home, power lines, or other structures become risks regardless of their health.

**Professional tree trimming** can often solve this problem without removal by selectively removing problematic limbs, crown-raising, or directional pruning. But trimming near power lines should only ever be done by a licensed professional — never attempt it yourself.

## Tree Trimming vs. Tree Removal: Which Do You Need?

Not every hazardous tree needs to come down. A professional arborist will evaluate:

- The percentage of the canopy that is dead or diseased
- The extent and location of structural defects
- The proximity to structures, power lines, and high-traffic areas
- The species and its inherent structural characteristics
- The value of the tree to the property

Often, targeted trimming and deadwood removal extends a tree's safe life by years. When removal is necessary, however, it should be done before the tree fails on its own terms.
