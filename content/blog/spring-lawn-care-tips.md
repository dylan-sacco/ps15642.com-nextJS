Spring arrives in Westmoreland County and every homeowner looks out the window at the same thing: a lawn that's brown, matted, and covered in whatever winter left behind. The good news is that cool-season lawns — the type most of us have in Western PA — are naturally resilient and bounce back quickly with the right care at the right time.

The bad news is that doing the right things at the wrong time can do more harm than good. Fertilizing too early, aerating wet soil, or mowing before the grass is ready all set your lawn back instead of forward.

Here's a step-by-step spring lawn care guide built for the Western Pennsylvania climate.

![Green lawn emerging after winter with early spring growth in a residential setting](https://picsum.photos/id/274/800/500)

## Step 1: Resist the Urge to Rush (March)

The biggest spring lawn care mistake Western PA homeowners make is starting too early. When you see the first green blades pushing through in late February or March, it's tempting to get out there and start raking, fertilizing, and mowing. Resist that urge.

**Wait until:**
- There's no more risk of hard frost (typically mid-April in Westmoreland County)
- The soil is dry enough that walking on it doesn't leave deep footprints
- Grass is actively growing, not just greening up from stored energy

Walking on saturated spring soil compacts it and damages root structure. Applying fertilizer before the grass is actively growing is wasteful at best and potentially damaging at worst.

## Step 2: Spring Cleanup (Early April)

Once the ground has dried and temperatures are consistently above 45°F, it's time for a thorough spring cleanup:

**Rake the entire lawn** — not just to remove leaves, but to break up matted grass that may have developed snow mold over winter. Snow mold creates a gray or pink fuzzy crust on the grass surface that prevents light and air from reaching new growth. A light raking breaks this up and allows the lawn to breathe.

**Look for:**
- **Vole damage** — winding trails of dead grass where voles tunneled under the snow all winter. These areas need overseeding
- **Ice damage** — low spots where ice sat for extended periods may have suffocated the grass
- **Salt damage** — along driveways and sidewalks where deicing products were used

Mark any problem areas so you can address them in the seeding step later.

## Step 3: Apply Pre-Emergent Crabgrass Preventer (Mid-April)

This is the most time-sensitive application of the entire lawn care year. **Pre-emergent herbicide must go down before crabgrass seeds germinate** — once they've sprouted, pre-emergent has no effect.

**The trigger:** Apply pre-emergent when soil temperatures reach 50°F at a 2-inch depth for several consecutive days. In Westmoreland County, this typically falls between April 10–25.

A practical timing cue used by many local lawn pros: apply pre-emergent when the forsythia bushes in your neighborhood are blooming — forsythia bloom coincides closely with the 50°F soil temperature threshold.

**Important:** If you need to overseed bare spots, know that most pre-emergent herbicides also prevent grass seed from germinating. You'll need to use a product labeled safe for overseeding, or plan overseeding for fall when pre-emergent has broken down.

## Step 4: First Fertilization (Late April)

Once grass is actively growing and soil temps have hit 55°F, apply a **balanced, slow-release spring fertilizer**. Look for a product with a moderate nitrogen ratio — something around 20-5-10 or similar. Slow-release nitrogen feeds the lawn gradually over 6–8 weeks rather than pushing a burst of soft, disease-prone growth.

**How much to apply:** Follow the bag's label based on your lawn's square footage. Over-applying nitrogen — especially fast-release formulations — causes excessive shoot growth that diverts energy from root development and increases disease susceptibility.

## Step 5: First Mowing (Late April – Early May)

The first mowing of the season sets the tone for the year. Do it right:

- **Set the blade at 3 to 3.5 inches** — never lower than 2.5 inches on cool-season grasses
- **Make sure your blade is sharp** — a dull blade tears grass instead of cutting it, leaving jagged edges that turn brown and allow disease entry
- **Don't remove more than one-third of the blade length** at any mowing

If the lawn has grown very long through the early spring, step down the height gradually over two or three mowings rather than scalping it all at once.

## Step 6: Overseed Bare or Thin Areas (May)

For areas too large to be effectively filled by pre-emergent application, wait until early May to overseed:

1. **Rough up the soil surface** with a rake or power rake to ensure seed-to-soil contact
2. **Apply quality grass seed** matched to your lawn type and sun conditions — a quality tall fescue blend works well for most Westmoreland County lawns
3. **Lightly top-dress** with a thin layer of starter soil or peat moss to retain moisture
4. **Water consistently** — new seed needs to stay moist until germination (typically 10–21 days)
5. **Apply starter fertilizer** (high in phosphorus) to support root development in new seedlings

## Step 7: Broadleaf Weed Control (May)

Dandelions, clover, plantain, and ground ivy are best treated in spring when they're actively growing and most susceptible to herbicide uptake. Apply a **selective broadleaf herbicide** labeled for use on lawns (these kill weeds without harming grass).

**Wait to apply until:**
- Daytime temperatures are consistently above 50°F
- You've completed at least one or two mowings (actively growing weeds absorb herbicide better)
- Rain isn't forecast for 24–48 hours after application

For persistent or widespread weed problems, a second application 4–6 weeks after the first is often more effective than a single heavy application.

| Task | Timing | Notes |
|------|--------|-------|

| Overseeding | Early May | Avoid where pre-emergent applied |
